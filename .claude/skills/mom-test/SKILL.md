# The Mom Test

Bad customer conversations are not merely useless. **They convince you that you
are right.** A false positive costs more than no data, because it is acted on.

The goal of a research conversation is **concrete facts about a person's life**
— what they did, when, what it cost them. Not opinions, not predictions, not
enthusiasm.

## The three rules

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** If they know what you are hoping to
   hear, they will say it.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or the future.** *"What did
   you do last Tuesday"* beats *"what do you usually do"* beats *"what would you
   do"*.
3. **Talk less.** The more you are talking, the worse it is going.

## Testing a question

A question is bad if the answer would be the same whether or not the problem is
real. Run each one through:

| Test | Fails if |
|---|---|
| **Does it ask about the past?** | It contains *would*, *will*, *might*, *could you see yourself*, *ever* |
| **Does it ask for a fact?** | It asks for an opinion, a rating, a prediction, or a preference |
| **Could the answer be no?** | The answer is *yes* for everyone in the segment, so it carries no information |
| **Do we already know it?** | It is answerable from analytics, a CRM, or a prior survey |
| **Does it trace to an objective?** | You cannot say which of your three big questions it serves |
| **Is it neutral?** | A word in it primes an answer — *consequences*, *problem*, *annoying*, *frustrating* |
| **Would an unexpected answer change anything?** | Nothing you do differs based on the answer. Then it is not important |

### Conversions

| Bad | Why | Better |
|---|---|---|
| *Do you think it's a good idea?* | Opinion. Only the market knows | *Show me how you do it today* |
| *Would you use / buy X?* | Future, and always yes | *What did you do the last time this came up? What did it cost you?* |
| *How much would you pay?* | A number makes a guess feel rigorous | *What are you paying now, for what, and to whom?* |
| *What's your biggest problem with X?* | Presumes X is a problem. Premature zoom | *What are the three things you are trying to fix right now?* — see if X appears |
| *Would that be helpful?* | Invites a compliment | *What are you doing about it at the moment?* |
| *On a scale of 1–5…* | Formality, and a number with no story behind it | *Tell me about the last time* |

### Good questions, unchanged

*Why do you bother?* · *What are the implications of that?* · *Talk me through
the last time that happened* · *What else have you tried?* · *How are you
dealing with it now?* · *Who else should I talk to?* · *Is there anything else I
should have asked?*

## Reviewing someone else's script

This checklist comes from real review comments on a real script. Every item was
a genuine miss.

- **"What are we trying to learn with this question?"** Ask it of every line. If
  the author cannot answer in a sentence, cut the question. This single question
  removes more bad script than any rule.
- **Cut anything the data already answers.** *How many properties do you manage*
  when they are a logged-in customer. *How many contacts do you have* when a
  survey already asked. Spend the conversation on what the database cannot say.
- **Cut anything whose answer is always yes.** *Has a guest ever messaged you a
  question?* Every host gets messages. Zero information.
  **Exception worth keeping:** if the *context* around the known fact is the
  point — where they were, what else they were doing, what it interrupted — then
  keep it and ask for the context explicitly.
- **"The last time" is not automatically representative.** It is the Mom Test's
  workhorse and it can still mislead if the most recent instance was atypical.
  Pair it: *the last time*, and *the most difficult one*, and *the most common
  one*. Three anchors, one topic.
- **Check every adjective for priming.** *"What were the consequences?"* pulls
  for something negative; *"what happened?"* does not. Prefer the emptier word.
- **Section headings smuggle assumptions.** A section called *Workarounds*
  presumes a workaround exists; *Their current process* does not. Internal
  headings shape the questions written under them.
- **Ask what new pain the solution would create**, not only whether the old pain
  is real. A research objective that can only confirm is not an objective.
- **Ask what they would NOT hand over.** The refusals define the product's edge
  more sharply than the requests.

## Structuring a script

    Context (for you, not read aloud)
      What are we trying to find out?
      Why does it matter — what decision changes?
      Risk check: product risk or market risk?
      The 3 big questions for this round

    Warm-up (2–3 min) — their life, no topic yet
    Current behaviour — what they do now, in specifics
    Their existing process — how, and why that way
    Cost — what it costs when it goes wrong
    Trust / refusals — what they would not give up
    Wrap-up — the commitment ask

**Exactly three big questions per round.** Pick the murkiest. They change every
round. You do not need to ask all three of every person.

### Risk check — do this before writing questions

- **Market risk** — *do they want it, will they pay, are there enough of them?*
  Conversations answer this. Spend the time here.
- **Product risk** — *can I build it, can I grow it, will they keep using it?*
  Conversations cannot answer this. *"If you build that, I'll use it"* tells you
  nothing you did not already know.

Naming which one you face decides how much research is worth doing at all.

## Commitment, including when nothing costs money

A conversation that ends in a compliment failed. It ends well when they give up
something they value. **Money is only one currency**, and for a free or consumer
product it is the wrong one — asking about price invents a hypothetical.

| Currency | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | *Keep me posted* | Thirty minutes now · a scheduled next session · using it for two weeks |
| **Reputation** | *I know people you should talk to* | An intro made today, by name |
| **Access** | *Sure, I'd try it* | Showing you their actual spreadsheet · importing their real contacts |
| **Money** | *I'd pay for that* | A deposit, a pre-order, a card on file |

Rule: **a fuzzy promise converted into a specific one is the whole ask.** *Who,
and why not now?*

## Bad data, and how to clear it

- **Compliments** — deflect and return to facts. *"That's really kind — how are
  you handling it at the moment?"*
- **Fluff** — generics (*usually, always, never*), futures (*I would, I will*),
  hypotheticals (*I might*). Anchor it: *when did that last happen? talk me
  through it.*
- **Ideas and feature requests** — record, never obey. Dig for the motivation:
  *what would that let you do? how are you coping without it?*

**There is more reliable information in a "meh" than a "Wow!"** A lukewarm
answer is a clean result. Upgrading your pitch until they say something nice
converts a good result into a false positive.

## Notes

One quote per card, verbatim, in quotation marks. Mark each with what it is:

`☇` pain · `⚑` goal · `☐` obstacle · `⤴` their current workaround ·
`^` context · `☑` feature request · `＄` money · `♀` a named person or company ·
`☆` follow-up · `:)` excited · `:(` angry · `:|` embarrassed

An emotion changes the meaning of everything beside it. *"That's a problem"* said
flatly and said furiously are different data.

## Signs the round is wasted

- You talked more than they did
- They complimented the idea
- You do not know what happens next
- An unexpected answer changed nothing
- None of your questions frightened you
- You cannot name which big question a conversation served

## Output when using this skill

When **writing** a script: produce the context block (objective, risk check,
three big questions) before any questions, then the sections above, then a
named commitment ask. Say which question serves which objective.

When **reviewing** a script: go line by line. For each question give
**keep / cut / rewrite**, the failing test by name, and the replacement. Flag
any question the author could not justify in one sentence.
